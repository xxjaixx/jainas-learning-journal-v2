When Fast Becomes Fragile: Rebuilding Student Apps with Secure Design Principles

Introduction

The Security Operations Center (SOC) has traditionally depended heavily on human analysts to monitor security alerts, investigate suspicious activity, and respond to cybersecurity incidents. However, the growing number of alerts and the increasing complexity of cyber threats are creating pressure for organizations to find faster and more scalable ways to operate their SOCs. In his IBM article, Velisaris (2025) presents an interesting vision of an autonomous SOC in which agentic artificial intelligence can perform many routine cybersecurity tasks while human analysts become more like "SOC pilots."

The comparison to a modern airliner is useful because pilots do not manually control every part of the aircraft during normal operations. Automated systems can handle many routine activities, while the pilot remains responsible for monitoring the situation and taking control when something unexpected happens. A similar approach could be applied to cybersecurity. AI agents could monitor systems, analyze alerts, and perform approved responses, while human analysts supervise the process and handle situations that require deeper judgment.

I believe the SOC pilot model is realistic, but only if organizations treat it as a human-AI partnership rather than complete replacement of human cybersecurity professionals. AI can provide speed and scale, but cybersecurity decisions often depend on context, consequences, and information that may not always be available to an AI system.

Can the SOC Pilot Model Really Work?

The idea of a SOC pilot is realistic because many SOC activities are repetitive and follow recognizable patterns. Security teams receive large numbers of alerts from firewalls, endpoint protection systems, authentication systems, network monitoring tools, and other security technologies. Analysts must determine which alerts are important and which ones can be safely ignored or handled through routine procedures.

This is an area where AI can provide significant assistance. An AI agent can continuously process large amounts of security information and identify patterns that may be difficult for a human analyst to notice immediately. It can also help organize alerts according to their possible severity and relevance.

The benefit is not only speed. AI can operate continuously without becoming tired or overwhelmed by the number of alerts. This could allow security teams to spend more time investigating serious incidents instead of manually processing every routine event.

However, the SOC pilot model should not be understood as giving AI unlimited control. A responsible implementation would require clear boundaries regarding which actions an AI agent is allowed to perform. Organizations should determine which actions are low-risk and reversible and which actions require approval from a human analyst.

Where AI Can Safely Act on Its Own

AI can safely perform certain cybersecurity activities when the task is repetitive, clearly defined, and supported by reliable information. One example is continuous monitoring. An AI system can examine security events and identify unusual patterns without requiring a human analyst to manually inspect every single event.

AI can also assist with alert triage. When hundreds or thousands of alerts are generated, an AI agent can help determine which events deserve immediate attention. This can reduce the workload of analysts and allow them to concentrate on the alerts that are more likely to represent real threats.

Another area where AI may safely operate is the execution of predefined and approved response procedures. For example, an organization may establish rules for handling a known type of suspicious activity. If an event clearly matches those conditions, an AI agent could potentially perform a limited response according to the organization's approved procedures.

These examples show that automation is most appropriate when the organization understands the task and has established clear limits. The AI should not simply decide that it can perform any action it wants. Its authority should be based on security policies, predefined procedures, and appropriate safeguards.

Why Context Matters

One of the most important ideas in the SOC pilot model is that detecting an alert is not the same as understanding the alert. An AI agent may recognize suspicious activity, but it still needs context before it can determine the appropriate response.

For example, imagine that an AI agent detects unusual activity from a device. The same technical alert could have very different consequences depending on what that device actually is. If it is an ordinary workstation, isolating it may have a limited effect on the organization. If it is a critical server supporting an important business service, however, automatically isolating it could cause a much more serious disruption.

This is why asset information is extremely important. The AI needs to understand what systems exist, how important they are, who uses them, and what their normal activities look like. Without this context, an AI agent may technically identify the correct event but still choose the wrong response.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 emphasizes the importance of managing cybersecurity risks across organizational activities and understanding the systems and risks that need to be protected (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). This supports the idea that effective automation requires more than simply detecting threats. It also requires reliable organizational and security context.

What Happens When Asset Information Is Wrong?

Incomplete or incorrect asset information can become one of the biggest weaknesses in an autonomous SOC. If an AI agent depends on asset information to decide how serious an alert is, incorrect information can lead to an incorrect decision.

For example, suppose a critical server is incorrectly recorded as an ordinary workstation. An AI agent may not understand that the system is essential to the organization. It could therefore assign the alert a lower priority than it deserves or apply a response that is not appropriate for such an important system.

The opposite problem is also possible. An ordinary workstation could be incorrectly classified as a critical system. The AI might then prioritize relatively minor activity and consume valuable security resources investigating something that does not require immediate attention.

The problem becomes more serious when the AI is allowed to take automatic action. Incorrect information could cause the system to block a legitimate user, isolate an important device, or interrupt a service that should remain available.

This demonstrates an important limitation of autonomous security systems: automation does not automatically correct bad information. Instead, automation can make decisions faster based on the information it receives. If that information is incomplete or wrong, the system may also make a wrong decision faster.

Because of this, organizations need processes for maintaining and validating asset information. AI should not be expected to compensate for inaccurate information about the environment it is protecting.

Where Human Judgment Must Remain

Even if AI becomes highly capable, there are situations where human judgment should remain essential. Major cybersecurity incidents are one example. If an incident could affect a critical system, important business operations, or a large number of users, the consequences of an incorrect automated response could be significant.

Human analysts should also remain involved when the available evidence is incomplete or contradictory. AI can analyze information quickly, but cybersecurity decisions sometimes require understanding the wider organizational situation. A human may know that a particular activity is related to approved maintenance, a business process, or an unusual but legitimate situation.

Human oversight is also important because cybersecurity decisions can involve accountability. If an automated action causes a major disruption, an organization needs to know who was responsible for establishing the rules and approving the level of authority given to the AI system.

The NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework emphasizes managing risks associated with AI systems and promoting trustworthy and responsible AI practices (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023). This supports the need for organizations to consider not only what an AI system can do, but also whether its use is reliable, secure, and appropriately governed.

For this reason, I do not think the best SOC model is "AI versus humans." A better model is AI working with humans. AI can handle speed, scale, and repetitive work, while human analysts remain responsible for difficult decisions and situations where the consequences are significant.

What Skills Would I Need to Become a SOC Pilot?

If AI becomes more involved in SOC operations, cybersecurity professionals will still be needed. However, their responsibilities may change. Instead of spending most of their time manually reviewing every alert, analysts may spend more time supervising AI systems, validating their decisions, and handling situations that require human judgment.

The first skill I would need is a strong foundation in cybersecurity. I would need to understand networks, authentication, access control, security monitoring, common attack techniques, and incident response. Without this knowledge, it would be difficult to determine whether an AI-generated recommendation is reasonable.

I would also need AI literacy. A SOC pilot should understand the capabilities and limitations of AI systems. This includes knowing that an AI recommendation is not automatically correct simply because it was generated quickly or based on a large amount of data.

Critical thinking would be another important skill. A SOC pilot should be able to question an AI decision and ask whether the evidence actually supports the recommended action. If an AI wants to isolate a device, for example, the analyst should be able to determine whether the device is truly compromised and whether isolating it could affect important operations.

Communication skills would also remain important. During a major cybersecurity incident, the SOC pilot may need to explain the situation to managers, system administrators, and other teams. The ability to communicate technical information clearly would therefore remain valuable even in a highly automated SOC.

Finally, I would need to develop the ability to work with automation rather than compete against it. Instead of thinking that AI will replace every cybersecurity professional, I would focus on becoming the person who knows how to supervise, question, and improve AI-assisted security operations.

Conclusion

The SOC pilot model is realistic, but I believe it is responsible only when automation is introduced with clear boundaries and human oversight. AI can provide major benefits by monitoring systems, analyzing large amounts of security information, prioritizing alerts, and performing approved routine actions.

However, AI cannot make responsible decisions without reliable context. If asset information is incomplete or incorrect, the AI may misunderstand the importance of a system and respond inappropriately. This is especially dangerous when automated actions could interrupt critical services or affect legitimate users.

Human analysts therefore still have an important role. Their role may change from manually handling every alert to supervising AI, validating important decisions, responding to unusual situations, and taking responsibility for high-impact actions.

For me, becoming a SOC pilot would mean developing both technical and human skills. I would need cybersecurity knowledge, AI literacy, critical thinking, incident response skills, and strong communication. The goal would not be to compete with AI but to understand how to use it responsibly.

AI may eventually take the wheel for many routine parts of security operations, but humans should still know where the organization is going, what could go wrong, and when it is necessary to take control. That is what makes the SOC pilot model not only realistic, but potentially responsible.

Looking Through an Auditor’s Eyes: Revisiting Our Risk Assessment Form

October 2026 • Blog Post 3 • 10 min read

Introduction

Revisiting our group's Risk Assessment Form through an auditor's perspective helped us understand that risk assessment is not only about identifying threats and assigning risk scores. It is also about determining what assets are important, who is responsible for them, what evidence exists, and whether the controls in place are actually working.

Looking at our previous work from an auditor's perspective allowed us to evaluate our assessment more critically. Instead of simply asking what risks were present, we had to ask whether the information we recorded could be supported by evidence and whether the organization could clearly demonstrate how its assets were protected.

To protect the confidentiality of the organization involved in our fieldwork, this reflection discusses our observations only in general terms. No employee names, credentials, internal configurations, or specific vulnerabilities are disclosed.

Understanding the Organization’s Most Important Assets

From an auditor's perspective, the first question should be: what assets are important to the organization? Assets are not limited to computers and physical equipment. They can include information, applications, network resources, records, facilities, and other resources that support business operations.

During our risk assessment, we realized that some assets are directly connected to the organization's ability to continue its daily operations. Devices such as laptops and other equipment can support employees in completing their work. Information and records can also be important because their loss, alteration, or unauthorized disclosure may affect the organization.

An auditor would therefore look beyond simply listing assets. The assessment should explain why each asset matters, what processes depend on it, and what could happen if it becomes unavailable, damaged, altered, or accessed without authorization.

Classifying Assets According to Their Importance

Not every asset has the same level of importance. An asset inventory becomes more useful when assets are classified according to their value and the consequences associated with their compromise.

For example, some assets may be primarily important for availability because employees depend on them to continue their work. Other assets may require stronger confidentiality protections because they contain sensitive information. Some assets may require protection of both confidentiality and integrity because unauthorized changes could affect business decisions or operations.

From an auditor's perspective, classification helps determine whether the security controls applied to an asset are appropriate for its importance. Higher-value or higher-risk assets may require stronger controls, closer monitoring, and more frequent review.

Establishing Clear Asset Ownership and Responsibility

Another important area an auditor would examine is asset ownership. Every important asset should have a responsible owner or department that understands its purpose, value, risks, and security requirements.

Without clear ownership, it becomes difficult to determine who should maintain an asset, review its access permissions, approve changes, or respond when something goes wrong. Responsibility can easily become unclear when an asset is shared by multiple people or departments.

An asset inventory should therefore include information about the responsible owner or custodian. This creates accountability and makes it easier for the organization to manage security requirements throughout an asset's lifecycle.

What Evidence Would an Auditor Require?

One of the biggest differences between completing a classroom risk assessment and thinking like an auditor is the importance of evidence. An auditor cannot rely only on verbal statements that a control exists.

For asset management, an auditor could examine an updated asset inventory showing the organization's assets, owners, classifications, and status. For access control, an auditor could review access lists, user accounts, or authorization records.

For backup controls, an auditor could request backup logs and evidence that restoration tests have been performed. For physical security, an auditor could observe whether restricted areas are actually protected.

This taught us that strong audit evidence should be specific, relevant, reliable, and verifiable. Actual records, logs, documents, observations, and test results provide stronger support than statements alone.

Gaps an Auditor Might Uncover

Looking at our Risk Assessment Form from an auditor's perspective also made us realize that an organization may have controls but still have gaps in documentation, accountability, or monitoring.

One possible gap is an incomplete asset inventory. If some assets are missing from the inventory, the organization may not know what needs protection or who is responsible for it.

Another possible gap is outdated ownership information. Employees may change roles, equipment may be transferred, and systems may be replaced. If the inventory is not regularly updated, the documented information may no longer represent the actual environment.

An auditor may also identify weaknesses when controls are documented but there is insufficient evidence showing that they are consistently performed. This demonstrates why documentation and evidence are important parts of effective security management.

Strengthening Risk Treatments Through an Asset Inventory and Audit Checklist

An accurate asset inventory can strengthen risk treatment because it gives the organization a clearer understanding of what needs protection. Instead of applying the same treatment to every asset, security measures can be prioritized based on asset value, classification, ownership, and risk.

An audit checklist can further strengthen this process by connecting a control requirement with an audit question, expected evidence, and the resulting finding.

For example, an asset management audit question could ask: "Does every important asset have an assigned owner?" The expected evidence could be the organization's asset inventory. For access control, the question could ask whether only authorized personnel can access sensitive resources, with access lists serving as evidence.

This approach makes the audit process more organized and helps ensure that recommendations are based on observable evidence rather than assumptions.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 emphasizes understanding and managing cybersecurity risks through activities such as Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. Similarly, security controls and audit activities help organizations determine whether their protections are appropriate and effective.

Conclusion

Revisiting our Risk Assessment Form through an auditor's perspective gave us a deeper understanding of information security. We learned that identifying risks is only one part of the process. Organizations must also know what assets they have, how those assets should be classified, who is responsible for them, and what evidence can demonstrate that security controls are working.

An asset inventory provides visibility and accountability, while an audit checklist provides a structured way to verify controls and collect evidence. Together, they can help organizations identify gaps and improve their risk treatments.

Most importantly, this activity showed us that auditing is not simply about finding mistakes. It is about understanding whether the organization's security practices are appropriate, supported by evidence, and capable of reducing risk.
